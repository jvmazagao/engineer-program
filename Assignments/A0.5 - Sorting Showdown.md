## Why

"Sorting is O(n log n)" is half a sentence. Which sort, on which input shape, with what memory? Quicksort on nearly-sorted data is the classic accidental O(n²), and knowing stability tells you when sorting a table twice breaks the first ordering.

## Brief

Create `dsa-from-scratch/04-sorting-showdown/` and implement:

1. **Insertion sort**
2. **Merge sort**
3. **Quicksort** — naive: pivot is the **last** element, Lomuto or Hoare partition, your choice (keep it naive on purpose)

Benchmark all three on three input shapes — **random**, **already sorted**, **reversed** — at n = 1 000, 5 000, 10 000 (insertion sort may crawl; that's the point — let it). Additionally run merge and quicksort at n = 100 000 on random input. Print one table: sort × shape × n.

Tests for each sort: empty, one element, duplicates, all-equal, already sorted, reversed, negative numbers.

Then revisit [[H-Index]]: solve it using your merge sort (sort citations, find h), and note in the submission what the O(n) counting-sort variant would look like.

## Tasks

- [ ] Implement the three sorts, tested against the case list above
- [ ] Benchmark script: 3 sorts × 3 shapes × 3 sizes (+ 100 000 for merge/quick random), table printed
- [ ] In the submission note, answer:
  - Feed your quicksort a sorted array with the last-element pivot. Walk the partitions: what sizes does each call get? What's the recursion depth and total cost?
  - Why is insertion sort *fast* on sorted input? What's its best case?
  - What's the memory cost of each sort?
  - Which of your three sorts are **stable**? Prove it with a test on `(key, payload)` pairs.
  - What algorithm does your language's builtin `sort` use, and is it stable?
