## Why

Databases indexes are trees, and "add an index" is the most common performance fix a senior ships. This assignment builds the tree by hand, then makes you explain the real thing — B-trees — to someone without a CS background, which is a daily senior skill.

## Brief

Create `dsa-from-scratch/05-bst-indexes/` with two parts.

**Part 1 — BST.** Implement a binary search tree with:

- `insert(key, value)`, `search(key)`, `delete(key)` (all three delete cases: leaf, one child, two children)
- State your duplicates policy and enforce it
- **Four traversals** returning lists: pre-order, in-order, post-order, level-order (BFS)
- Tests: empty tree, single node, sorted input (watch what the tree becomes), duplicates, delete root / leaf / internal node, and **an in-order traversal of any valid BST must come out sorted** — test that

**Part 2 — `INDEXES.md`, one page.** An explainer for a non-CS colleague (a PM, a support engineer, a junior QA):

- Why reading a table row-by-row is slow as data grows
- What an index is: a sorted structure that holds keys + pointers to rows
- Why databases use **B-trees** and not binary trees: draw the height math — a binary tree over 10 million rows is ~23 levels deep (one disk read each); a B-tree with branching factor ~500 is ~3 levels. Keep it concrete, no jargon.
- What indexes make fast (equality and range lookups, sorting) and what they cost (slower writes, extra storage)
- One paragraph: "when is an index the wrong answer?"

Plain language throughout. If a sentence needs a CS degree, rewrite it.
